Two ideas, taught side by side, that turn out to be the same idea. Neither column is a metaphor for the other — they are the same arithmetic in different clothes. If you already know both, skip ahead to the examples.

What this page is for

Later on you will be asked to believe a specific number: that a service reporting 99 % accessibility can still fail roughly a quarter of the people who try to use it. That claim is arithmetic, not opinion — but the arithmetic only lands if two ideas already make sense. So this page teaches both at once, a step at a time.

Left column — permutations: how independent things combine, and why they combine by multiplying rather than averaging. Right column — digital accessibility: who is affected, what the standard actually requires, and why "one page passed" is not "the task works."

The joining idea. A permutation is what you get when independent things combine, and independence means the probabilities multiply. A public-service journey is a permutation of its steps: 30 interactions in sequence, each with its own chance of failing the person using it. That is the entire argument. Everything below is that sentence, unpacked slowly.

Beat 1 — what a permutation is

Counting arrangements of distinct things

A permutation is an arrangement of things where order matters. Counting them answers a plain question: how many ways can this turn out?

Two coins, each heads or tails, give four possible arrangements:

HH HT TH TT arrangements = 2 × 2 = 4

Three coins give 2³ = 8. Ten coins give 2¹⁰ = 1,024. The count multiplies, it never adds — and that single fact is the whole page.

Beat 1 — what accessibility is

A requirement that applies to every person, at once

Digital accessibility means a service works for everyone who needs it, including people with disabilities — and the obligation is not averaged over a crowd, it applies to each user individually.

Roughly 1 in 4 US adults reports a disability (CDC BRFSS 2022: 28.7 %). The US Census measures a narrower definition and finds 13.6 % of all ages (ACS 2024: 45.8 million people).

That disagreement is the first real lesson. Two credible official sources differ by more than a factor of two — because they ask different questions. Every number in this artefact therefore states its source and its definition. If you want to attack the conclusion later, attack it there.

Beat 2 — independence

When one outcome doesn't change the next

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other. A coin landing heads does not make the next coin more likely to be tails.

For independent events, the chance that both happen is the product:

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) two coins, both heads: ½ × ½ = ¼ = 25 %

Notice what this does to a chain: if each step succeeds with probability p, then n independent steps succeed together with probability pⁿ. Small per-step losses become large journey losses.

Beat 2 — the four principles

POUR: four obligations that all apply at once

WCAG 2.2 organises its requirements under four principles. A service must satisfy all four — this is the accessibility version of an "and":

  • PerceivableCan I receive the information? (text alternatives, contrast, captions)
  • OperableCan I use the controls? (keyboard, timing, target size, no drag-only)
  • UnderstandableCan I follow and predict it? (labels, errors, consistent navigation)
  • RobustDoes it work with my technology? (names, roles, values, compatibility)

A page that is perceivable but not operable fails. There is no partial credit, because the user needs all four — the same "and" as the coin above.

Beat 3 — the multiplication bites

Why "99 % each" is not "99 % overall"

Suppose each of 30 steps succeeds with probability 0.99. Independence means we multiply:

P(all 30 succeed) = 0.99³⁰ ≈ 0.7397 so the chance of at least one failure ≈ 26.03 %

The vendor's monitoring reports each step at 99 %. The citizen experiences the product, not the average. Nobody is served by the average of 30 steps — a person meets the steps in order and is stopped by whichever one fails them.

Beat 3 — a journey is a serial system

Every step has to work, for every person

Applying for a benefit is a sequence: screen eligibility, prove identity, enter finances, upload documents, sign, confirm. Thirty interactions across four stages. You cannot skip one and still be approved.

That makes the task what reliability engineers call a serial system: the whole thing works only if every part works. Component reliability multiplies; it never averages. This is mathematically identical to a chain of hardware that fails if any link fails.

A dashboard checks each part. The citizen lives the whole chain.

Beat 4 — permutations of kind, not just of order

Who a person is, crossed with what the build does

There is a second permutation in play. A citizen is a combination of capability axes, and each axis has levels. Using the Microsoft Persona Spectrum:

  • Vision — permanent (blind), temporary (post-treatment), situational (screen glare)
  • Motor/touch — permanent (keyboard-only), temporary (one-handed injury), situational (holding a toddler)
  • Cognition — permanent, temporary (migraine), situational (crisis and time pressure)
  • Hearing/speech — permanent (Deaf), temporary (laryngitis), situational (noisy transit)

Cross those axes with the build's defects and you get the space later pages sample from: 4 axes × 3 levels in each, times every step that can fail.

Beat 4 — disability is not a fixed minority

Temporary and situational, and a curve that reaches almost everyone

The Persona Spectrum exists because "disability" is not a static category. It shifts with injury, illness, situation and age:

  • 12 % of US adults ages 18–64 report a disability.
  • 24 % of those 65–74.
  • 46 % of those 75 and older (Census ACS / Pew 2023).

So the affected population is not a small fixed slice you can average away — it is a growing share of everyone, and any given person passes through it. The same barrier is met permanently by some, temporarily by others, and situationally by many more. That is why the model treats all three levels as real.

Beat 5 — combining more than two

Chains of chance, and how fast they collapse

The rule generalises: for n independent steps each succeeding with probability p, the whole chain succeeds with probability pⁿ.

Journey success for 30 steps at three per-step success rates
Per stepFormulaWhole journey
99.9 %0.999³⁰97.04 %
99 %0.99³⁰73.97 %
97 %0.97³⁰40.10 %

Improving each step from 99 % to 99.9 % — a tenfold cut in defects — buys about 23 percentage points of journey completion. That is the power of working on the exponent instead of the headline.

Beat 5 — why a green dashboard tells you little

Automation sees a slice of the standard

Automated tools — Lighthouse, axe-core, WAVE — are genuinely useful, and they agree on roughly how much they can decide: the GSA puts it near 30 % of success criteria; Deque puts it near 40 % of issues. The rest need a person who knows the standard to look.

WebAIM's 2025 crawl of the top million home pages found 51 detectable errors per page and 94.8 % of pages failing WCAG 2. Those are the errors automation can see. The blockers that stop people — keyboard traps, drag-only uploads, unannounced live updates — are largely in the part it cannot.

A perfect score means "nothing this tool knows how to check is broken." It does not mean the task works.

Beat 6 — where this page has been going

The same arithmetic, applied to people

Now combine the two columns. Thirty steps, each failing a given citizen with some probability, and a citizen who is a permutation of capability axes. The journey either works for that person or it does not.

per-step pass 0.99 → journey pass 73.97 % per-step pass 0.999 → journey pass 97.04 %

And that is before adding the barriers that stop someone outright while leaving the arithmetic untouched — the drag-only upload that has no keyboard path at all.

Beat 6 — what "blocked" actually means

Not every failure is the same kind of failure

  • Total blocker — no path exists. A drag-only upload with no keyboard alternative. The journey ends, deterministically, for those who cannot drag.
  • Partial blocker — a path exists for some of the group but not all. A date-picker that works with a pointer and not a keyboard.
  • Degrader — blocks nobody outright, but raises error and abandonment rate. Low contrast is the most common on the web and the most survivable.

Two of these are measured in the next page's population model; one of them — contrast — is real, reportable, and the reason nobody fails. Conflating them is how "99 %" gets published.

Where the two ideas meet. A permutation multiplies because independent things must all happen. A journey multiplies because every step must work for the person and all four POUR principles must hold. One arithmetic, two vocabularies. Once that clicks, the simulation is just bookkeeping.

Feel it: a running example at 1 %

Reading "0.99³⁰" is abstract. Clicking through it is not. Below is a short journey — a benefit application, the same shape as the full 30-step task — where every step has a 1 % chance of going wrong. That is the vendor's number: 99 % per component.

Press "Attempt step 1" and keep going. A 1 % failure rate will mostly pass. Occasionally it will not — and what happens next depends on what kind of defect it was, which is the second half of the argument. Watch the running total, and notice that it never comes back down.

The honest note the whole project rests on. At 1 %, most single runs pass every step. That is exactly why the problem is invisible to the person checking one page, and invisible to the vendor reporting one number. The injury is not in any single run — it is in the 26 % of journeys. Run it a few times and watch the odds turn against you.

    Where the running example's defects come from

    Each step is a real defect class, cited. The probability that any given run hits a particular one is 1 %, because the build is "99 % accessible" — the point is not that these are likely, it is that a multi-step journey gives failure many chances to happen, and that the three failure types produce completely different endings.
